There's a specific kind of chaos that happens when a crumble comes out of the oven right. The streusel cracks a little when you dig the spoon in. The peaches underneath are soft and syrupy, not mushy, not stiff. And the whole kitchen smells like cinnamon and browned butter. That's what this peach crumble recipe gets you, and it takes less effort than you'd think.

This isn't a fussy pastry project. There's no dough to chill, no lattice to weave, no blind baking anything. You mix a streusel, you mix a filling, you layer them, and you bake. If you've got 15 minutes of active time and a working oven, you've got this.

### **Why This Peach Crumble Recipe Works**

Canned peaches are the move here, and that's not a shortcut you need to apologize for. Rinsing off the syrup first keeps the filling from turning into peach soup, which is the number one way a crumble goes wrong. You still get real peach flavor. You just get it without babysitting a pot of fresh fruit for 20 minutes first.

The streusel is where this recipe earns its "*ultimate*" title. Dark brown sugar instead of light gives it a deeper, almost caramel edge. And there's a lot of it, more than most recipes call for, because a crumble without enough topping is really just baked peaches with a light dusting on top. Nobody wants that.

### **What You'll Need**

**For the Streusel**

* 2/3 cup dark brown sugar, packed
* 2 tablespoons white sugar
* 1 1/3 cups all-purpose flour
* 10 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted

**For the Peach Filling**

* Two 29-ounce cans of peaches, drained and rinsed
* 1/2 cup brown sugar
* 1 tablespoon vanilla extract
* 1 tablespoon ground cinnamon
* 1/2 tablespoon cornstarch

That's it. Nine ingredients, and you probably have most of them in your pantry right now.

### **How to Make Peach Crumble**

* #### **Step 1: Build the Streusel First**

Start with the streusel, not the filling. It needs time to firm up in the freezer, so getting it going early saves you a step later.

In a bowl, combine the dark brown sugar, white sugar, and flour. Give it a rough mix so everything's evenly distributed. Pour in the melted butter and mix until it comes together into a shaggy, clumpy texture. Don't overwork it into a paste. You want texture, not dough.

This makes a generous batch. If a mountain of streusel isn't your thing, freeze the extra. It holds up well and works on muffins, other fruit crumbles, basically anything that could use a crunchy, buttery topping later.

Pop the streusel in the freezer for about 10 minutes while you move on to the filling.

* #### **Step 2: Make the Peach Filling**

Drain your canned peaches and rinse them thoroughly. This part matters more than it sounds like it should. Canned peach syrup is sweet and thin, and if it goes into your crumble along with the fruit, you'll end up with a soggy, watery mess instead of a filling that holds its shape.

Once your peaches are rinsed and sitting in a bowl, add the brown sugar, vanilla extract, cinnamon, and cornstarch. Mix until every piece of fruit is coated. The cornstarch is doing quiet, unglamorous work here. It thickens the juices as the crumble bakes so you get a filling with body instead of a puddle.

* #### **Step 3: Layer and Top**

Pull your streusel out of the freezer. Ten minutes is usually enough for it to firm up into crumbly little clusters instead of a wet paste. If it's still soft, give it a few more minutes.

Spread your peach filling into a baking dish in an even layer. Then top it with the streusel, breaking it into pieces with your hands as you go so you get a mix of larger crumbles and smaller bits. Larger pieces crisp up nicely and give you something to actually bite into. Cover the peaches generously. This recipe makes enough streusel that you can go heavy without running out.

* #### **Step 4: Bake**

Bake at 375°F for about 40 minutes. You're watching for the streusel to turn a deep golden brown and firm up with a bit of crunch. Not dark brown, not burnt. Golden.

Every oven runs a little different, so start checking around the 35-minute mark. Once the top looks toasted and the filling is bubbling slightly at the edges, it's done.

* #### **Step 5: Cool Slightly and Serve**

Let the crumble sit for a few minutes before serving. The filling needs a moment to settle, and it's still going to be plenty warm.

Serve it with a scoop of vanilla ice cream. This isn't optional in my opinion. The contrast between the warm cinnamon peaches and the cold, melting ice cream is most of the point of making a crumble in the first place.

### **Tips for the Best Peach Crumble**

**Don't skip rinsing the peaches**. It feels like an extra step you could skip when you're hungry and impatient. Skip it anyway and you'll be fishing a watery crumble out of the oven, wondering what went wrong.

**Freeze extra streusel instead of tossing it.** You'll thank yourself the next time you want a fast dessert and don't feel like starting from scratch.

**Break the streusel into varied sizes**. A crumble topping made of uniform crumbs bakes evenly, sure, but it also bakes boring. Bigger clusters give you crunch. Smaller bits fill in the gaps. You want both.

**Watch the color, not the clock**. Forty minutes is a solid guideline, but ovens lie. Golden brown streusel is the real signal that your crumble is ready.

### **Can You Make This With Fresh Peaches?**

Yes, and if peaches are in season where you are, fresh fruit will give you a slightly brighter flavor. You'll need to peel and slice about 6 to 8 medium peaches to replace the canned fruit, and you may want to bump the cornstarch up slightly since fresh peaches release more juice as they bake. But honestly, canned peaches are what make this recipe fast and reliable year round, and there's no shame in reaching for the can.

### **How to Store Leftover Peach Crumble**

Cover it and keep it in the fridge for up to four days. Reheat individual portions in the microwave for about 30 seconds, or warm the whole dish in a 350°F oven for 10 to 15 minutes if you want the streusel to crisp back up. It won't be quite as crunchy as fresh out of the oven, but it's still good. Really good, actually.

### **Crumble vs. Cobbler vs. Crisp: What's the Difference?**

People throw these words around like they're the same dessert, and honestly, the lines get blurry. But there's a real distinction. A cobbler has a biscuit-like topping, sometimes dropped in spoonfuls, sometimes rolled and cut. A crisp usually includes oats in the topping, giving it a chewier, more textured bite. A crumble sticks to flour, sugar, and butter, with nothing else mixed in, which is exactly what gives this peach crumble recipe its clean, buttery snap.

None of these are better than the others. They're just different tools for the same job: turning soft, cooked fruit into something you'd actually want to eat with a spoon. This recipe happens to be a crumble because that streusel topping is doing all the heavy lifting, and there's nothing here competing with the peach and cinnamon flavor underneath.

### **Why Rinsing Canned Peaches Actually Matters**

It's worth saying again, because it's the single most common reason a crumble turns out wrong. Canned peaches usually come packed in a syrup that's mostly sugar water, sometimes light, sometimes heavy. That syrup is fine for eating peaches straight out of the can. It is not fine for baking.

If that syrup goes into your filling along with the fruit, you're adding a huge amount of extra liquid the cornstarch was never designed to handle. The result is a crumble that pools at the bottom of the dish instead of holding together. A quick rinse under running water, then a good drain, solves this before it starts. It takes maybe 90 seconds and it's the difference between a filling with real body and one that's basically peach soup with a hat on.

### **Frequently Asked Questions**

#### **Can I use light brown sugar instead of dark?**

You can, but you'll lose some of that deep, molasses-forward flavor that makes this streusel stand out. Dark brown sugar is worth seeking out for this one.

#### **What size baking dish should I use?**

A 9x13 dish works well for this amount of filling and streusel. A deep 9-inch square dish also works if you prefer a taller crumble.

#### **Can I make this ahead of time?**

You can assemble the filling and streusel separately, refrigerate the filling, and keep the streusel frozen. Combine and bake when you're ready to serve. Fresh out of the oven is always going to be the best version, but this makes hosting a lot less stressful.

#### **Why is my streusel more like a paste than crumbs?**

Your butter is probably too warm, or you mixed it too long. Melted butter should bring the flour and sugar together into loose clumps, not a smooth dough. If it turns pasty, pop it in the freezer a little longer before baking. It'll firm back up.

#### **Can I double this peach crumble recipe for a crowd?**

Yes. Just use a larger baking dish, like a deep 9x13 or two separate dishes, and add a few extra minutes to the bake time. Keep an eye on the streusel color rather than sticking rigidly to the clock, since a bigger dish takes longer to heat through evenly.

#### **Is this recipe freezer-friendly after baking?**

The baked crumble itself doesn't freeze especially well since the streusel loses its crunch once thawed. What does freeze well is the raw streusel, so that's the part worth making ahead and stashing away for next time.

### **Conclusion**

A good peach crumble recipe doesn't need to be complicated to be memorable. This one leans on canned peaches, a generous streusel, and a handful of pantry staples to get you something that tastes like it took a lot more effort than it did. Make it once and you'll understand why it's worth keeping in regular rotation, not just for special occasions.
